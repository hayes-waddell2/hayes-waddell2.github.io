Does Taking Strike Three Help Hitters?

Patient two-strike hitters chase less and walk more, but it does it lead to increased production?
Published

August 26, 2026

View GitHub Repo

Introduction

Every ballplayer grows up with the same coaching: with two-strikes you have to protect the plate and fight off anything close. The idea that striking out looking is somehow worse than striking out swinging (although obviously that isn’t the only possible outcome when swinging with two strikes) is ingrained in you at a young age. But throughout my own college career I noticed that I started performing better in two-strike counts when I became more patient. I did not want to strikeout looking, but I started chasing less and making higher quality contact when I took more borderline pitches. Was I actually onto something, maybe this worked for me but won’t for others, or did it just feel like this was working? In this analysis I set out to find out whether this idea holds up across Major League hitters.

Data

To test this I pulled pitch-level data for MLB hitters from 2021-2025 using Baseball Savant’s Statcast search feature. With this data I built a set of strikeout-composition metrics: overall strikeout rate, strikeout looking rate, and strikeout swinging rate (further divided into chase vs in zone), all expressed as a rate of plate appearances. I looked at this in two ways: across players, to see how patient hitters compare to aggressive ones, and within player, tracking the same hitter across multiple seasons to see if changes in two-strike swing behavior actually affected their results. From there, I advanced the analysis into how looking-strikeout rate interacts with these metrics in two-strike counts: chase rate, walk rate, and barrels per batted ball event. Barrel rate was chosen specifically because it reflects the batted ball contact only, walks and strikeouts don’t factor into the metric. Together, these metrics test the same question from two angles, can a hitter change their underlying behavior in two-strike counts, and if that change translates into better results at the plate.

Players Who Strikeout Looking More Chase Less

Across the five year sample of MLB hitters who saw at least 250 two-strike pitches, hitters who took strike three more often swung at less pitches out of the zone with two strikes (r = -0.65, Figure 1, left). But across all hitters, this strong relationship might only be explaining inherent differences between patient and aggressive hitters. To test that, I tracked individual hitters across multiple seasons, using a fixed effects regression to strip out each player’s average tendencies and isolate their year-to-year changes independent of league wide tendencies or their general approach. Within the same player, every 1 percentage-point rise in looking strikeout rate is associated with a 0.66 percentage-point decrease in two-strike chase rate (p < 0.001). Figure 2 (left) illustrates this relationship using each hitter’s values demeaned against their own average. This plot is only a visual approximation of the fixed-effects results since the plotted correlation does not include the year effects controlled for in the model. These results support that this trend isn’t just a fixed trait between different types of hitters, but it’s actually an adjustable behavior an individual hitter can act on (easier said than done).

Does This Improve Two-Strike Outcomes?

More Barrels?

Does chasing less pitches by being more prone to striking out looking actually make a player a better hitter, or just a more patient one? Barrel rate offers a clean way to test this, as it isolates raw contact quality from plate discipline. Across all hitters, looking strikeout rate correlates with an increase in two-strike barrel rate (r = 0.30, p <0.001, Figure 1, right). But this relationship is not coming from chasing less. When both chase rate and looking strikeout rate are included together in a regression predicting barrel rate chase rate contributes essentially nothing (p = 0.81). The best explanation is most likely that all of these results, across hitters, are just describing patient vs aggressive hitters in general.

Using the same fixed-effects approach as before, a hitter’s own year-to-year changes in looking strikeout rate showed no significant relationship with their own barrel rate (Figure 2, right). This was the least significant relationship within the analysis and the model essentially explained none of the within-player variation in barrel rate (\(R^2\) < 0.001). These results show that patient hitters tend to barrel the ball more, but that is because of a player’s inherent abilities not something an individual player can expect to do by taking more borderline pitches.

Less Chase Means More Walks

Across all hitters, chase rate and looking strikeout rate both help to explain two-strike walk rate. As one would logically assume, chasing more with two strikes leads to fewer walks in two-strike counts (\(\beta\) = -0.43, p = <0.001). Looking strikeout rate is a more interesting relationship. On its own patient hitters walk more (r = 0.41, Figure 1, middle), but when chase rate is controlled for in the regression model, an increase in looking strikeout rate is associated with fewer walks (\(\beta\) = -0.12, p <0.001).

Every 1 percentage-point increase in a player’s own looking strikeout rate is associated with a 0.26 percentage-point increase in their two-strike walk rate (p < 0.001). This suggests a real, adjustable behavior for a hitter over time. Although this increase in walks does not cover the increase in strikeouts. Controlling for a hitter’s general strikeout proneness, every 1 percentage-point increase in a player’s own looking strikeout rate is associated with a 0.69 percentage-point widening of their K%-BB% gap (p < 0.001). Figure 3 showcases that although two-strike walk rate increases as looking strikeout rate increases overall strikeout rate increases at a faster rate.

Was I Onto Something?

Thinking back on my own experience I made the decision to protect against certain parts of the zone/pitches with two strikes and to be okay with striking out looking. Which lead to more barrels (I think) with two strikes. Based on the results on this analysis though it seems I may have had the causal arrow backwards. A hitter’s real adjustable behavior is chasing less, or at least attempting to, and taking more strikeouts looking is a downstream effect of this and does not in itself drive better outcomes.

Patient hitters, as a group, chase less, walk more, barrel the ball more often. Striking out looking more is not the reason those things happen its just a result of the player type. But when the same player changes their behavior from one season to the next only some of those patterns hold up. Notably, chasing less is a real adjustable behavior season to season and it leads to more walks, but also more strikeouts. But making the adjustment to chase less with two-strikes has no effect on each player’s ability to barrel balls in two-strike counts. The contact-quality improvement I experienced almost certainly wasn’t coming from a decision to strikeout looking more, it was because I became a more selective hitter overall and physically a better hitter.

While these results offer some real, statistically significant insights into what a hitter can control with two strikes none of it is strong enough to override the differences in raw ability between players.
